# Competition F1D Model Airplane

## Summary

This was a three-year project where I designed, built, and tested lightweight rubber-powered airplanes for Science Olympiad Flight. A big focus for me was making the plane as consistent and repeatable as possible, so I designed my own CAD files, laser-cut balsa components, custom assembly jigs, and 3D-printed propeller jigs instead of relying only on stock parts. I then tested different propeller designs, rubber torque, center of gravity, shimming, and aircraft trim to figure out what actually improved the plane rather than just changing things randomly.

## Key Results

- **2nd place nationally in 2023** and **4th place nationally in 2022**
- Improved overall flight performance by approximately **50%** through repeated testing and design changes
- Built a setup to consistently measure **rubber motor winds and torque** between tests
- Used CAD, laser cutting, and 3D printing to make components more accurate and repeatable

## Three Years of Design Evolution

Over three seasons, my aircraft designs changed significantly as the event rules shifted and as I became more willing to experiment with custom ideas. Each year taught me something different: first how to build a repeatable system, then how to push performance further, and finally how to test more ambitious design ideas.

### Design Evolution

**2022 — Building a Repeatable System**: My first season focused on learning how the aircraft responded to changes in rubber torque, trim, and center of gravity. I started using a torque meter and more consistent winding procedures, which helped me get much more repeatable flights. *4th nationally.*

**2023 — Pushing the Design Further**: I became much more willing to move away from standard designs, experimenting with custom airframes, rubber densities, and a flaring propeller. After extensive testing, the custom setup became competitive with more conventional designs. *2nd nationally.*

**2024 — More Aggressive Experimentation**: I pushed further into custom wing geometry, including dihedral. The added complexity made the plane harder to manufacture and tune, and it ultimately performed worse than my previous design, but it taught me how important it is to balance ambitious ideas with enough time for testing.

### Engineering Challenge

- The goal was to build a rubber-powered airplane that could stay in the air as long as possible while still meeting each year's size and weight restrictions.
- The airplane weighed around 8 g, so small changes in wing geometry, center of gravity, angle of attack, propeller pitch, rubber motor setup, and trim could completely change how it flew.
- Because so many variables interacted with each other, I focused heavily on making the airplane and testing process as consistent as possible so I could isolate what was actually improving performance.

### Airframe Design & Manufacturing

- Each year started with laying out the wing, horizontal stabilizer, and fuselage geometry in CAD based on that year's dimensional rules.
- I selected balsa based on density and combined it with carbon fiber and lightweight film to keep the structure as light as possible while still maintaining enough stiffness.
- Because my school had limited fabrication resources, I sent the CAD designs out to be laser cut, which also helped make each build more consistent.
- I designed and 3D printed jigs to help control things such as wing washout, propeller geometry, film placement, and assembly alignment.
- Making the airframe repeatable was important because I wanted to test one variable at a time instead of having manufacturing differences affect the results.

### Propulsion System

- The propeller and rubber motor had to work together as one system. More torque did not automatically mean a better flight if too much energy was used during the initial climb.
- I experimented with different rubber motor setups, winding conditions, propeller pitches, and blade designs to balance initial climb performance with efficiency later in the flight.
- I used a torque meter and digital winding counter to make the starting conditions of each flight more repeatable.
- I used stretch winding, starting several feet away from the airplane and gradually walking the rubber motor back in as torque increased.
- One of my biggest experiments was a custom flaring propeller. My first versions performed poorly, so I repeatedly adjusted blade thickness, flexibility, pitch, and rubber motor setup.
- I also designed a 3D-printed jig to help keep the propeller pitch consistent between versions.
- After repeated testing, the flaring propeller became one of the major design changes on my 2023 aircraft that placed 2nd nationally.

### Flight Testing & Optimization

- My general testing process was: **Wind → Measure Torque → Fly → Observe → Adjust → Repeat**
- I tried to keep most of the airplane consistent while changing one main variable at a time.
- Variables I tested included center of gravity, angle of attack, decalage, wing and stabilizer shims, rubber motor setup, propeller pitch, propeller geometry, and wing geometry.
- The flight profile I was looking for was a slow and steady climb without stalling, followed by a stable cruise and gradual descent.
- Small changes could noticeably affect the flight, so balancing the whole system became more important than finding the "best" value for one individual variable.

### Experimental Designs

- As I became more comfortable with the event, I became more willing to move away from the standard designs most teams were using.
- There was not much detailed information available for some of the ideas I wanted to test, so I spent a lot of time looking through indoor free-flight resources and reaching out to experienced builders around the country.
- During my third season, I experimented with a more aggressive dihedral wing because I wanted to improve stability in disturbed air.
- The design was much harder to manufacture and tune consistently than I expected.
- I ultimately did not have enough testing time to fully optimize it before competition, and it did not outperform my previous design.

### What I Learned

- Some of my best results came from being willing to move away from standard designs, especially with the flaring propeller.
- At the same time, my third year showed me that adding complexity without enough testing time can make the overall design worse.
- Over time, I became much more focused on controlling variables, making designs repeatable, and understanding why something worked instead of just seeing that it worked.
