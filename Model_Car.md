# Model Car & Launch System

## Summary
I designed and built a gravity-powered vehicle and custom pulley launch system for a competition where the goal was to reach a target as quickly and accurately as possible without cracking an egg carried by the vehicle. I first planned the system in CAD and went through several design iterations, using interchangeable components so I could test different vehicle configurations without rebuilding the entire car. A large part of the project was balancing speed, stopping accuracy, friction, weight, mechanical advantage, and material selection to create a system that was both fast and repeatable.

### Key Results
- Designed and fabricated a complete **gravity-powered vehicle and pulley launch system**
- Balanced competing requirements of **speed, stopping accuracy, and egg protection**
- Created **interchangeable CAD-designed components** to rapidly test different configurations
- Used **3D printing** to prototype custom components and reduce rebuild time between iterations
- Applied principles of **mechanical advantage, pulleys, friction, and weight distribution** throughout the design

## How It Worked
- The launcher used gravitational potential energy to accelerate the vehicle through a pulley system.
- Once released, the vehicle traveled independently toward the target while carrying an egg.
- The design had to balance launch speed with straight-line tracking, stopping accuracy, and a low enough impact to keep the egg intact.

## Vehicle Design Iterations
I went through several vehicle layouts before settling on the final design. This CAD model was the first idea I came up with to fabricate. I used CAD to test different chassis lengths, wheel placements, and component layouts before building anything physically. I realized that after starting to build, it would be incredibly difficult to change parts for testing so one of my main goals after that was making parts interchangeable so I could test different configurations without rebuilding the entire vehicle.

## Vehicle Testing
- The competition was not just about making the vehicle fast. The car also had to stop as close as possible to a target point while carrying an egg without cracking it, so every design change had to balance **speed, accuracy, and impact**.
- I tested the vehicle repeatedly while changing one major variable at a time. Because I designed several parts to be interchangeable, I could quickly swap configurations instead of rebuilding the entire vehicle between tests.

### Variables I Tested
- **Vehicle geometry**: changed chassis length and layout to improve stability and stopping behavior.
- **Weight distribution**: adjusted mass placement to improve straight-line tracking and consistency.
- **Wheel & axle alignment**: reduced drift that could cause the vehicle to miss the target.
- **Friction**: reduced unnecessary losses while keeping the vehicle predictable.
- **Launch setup**: adjusted the pulley and release configuration to improve launch consistency.

### Testing Process
- **Configure → Launch → Measure Time & Distance → Observe → Adjust → Repeat**
- Instead of only looking for the fastest run, I looked for a setup that could repeatedly reach nearly the same stopping point. A slightly faster vehicle was not useful if the stopping distance changed significantly between runs.
- The interchangeable parts became especially useful during this stage because I could quickly test different configurations while keeping most of the vehicle unchanged.

## Outcome & Takeaways
- This was probably my first project where I really went through the whole engineering process myself. I started with an idea, researched different designs, CAD everything out, built it, tested it, and then went back and changed things when they didn't work.
- The biggest thing I learned was how different designing something in CAD is from actually trying to build it. There were a lot of parts that I thought would work perfectly on the computer but just did not make sense once I actually had to fabricate and assemble them.
